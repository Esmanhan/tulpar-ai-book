What is Artificial Intelligence?

You will learn: common definitions of AI, the Turing test, narrow vs. general AI, and the key moments in AI history.

Aru: “Tulpar, are you intelligent?”

Tulpar: “That depends on what you mean by ‘intelligent’. Scientists have argued about that for seventy years. Let me show you the four main answers.”

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is the field of computer science that builds systems able to do tasks that normally require human intelligence: perceiving, reasoning, learning, planning, understanding language and making decisions. The term was coined by John McCarthy in the 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Summer Research Project, held in 1956. That workshop is usually counted as the birth of AI as a field.

Russell and Norvig, authors of the standard AI textbook, sort definitions along two questions: does the system think or act, and do we measure it against humans or against an ideal standard of rationality?

Measured against humansMeasured against rationality
ThinkingThinking humanly: model how people think (cognitive science)Thinking rationally: “laws of thought”, correct logical reasoning
ActingActing humanly: behave so that people cannot tell it is a machine (Turing test)Acting rationally: the rational agent approach. Do the action expected to give the best outcome. Modern AI mostly follows this view.

The Turing test

In 1950 Alan Turing published Computing Machinery and Intelligence. Instead of asking “Can machines think?”, he proposed the imitation game: a human judge chats in text with a hidden human and a hidden machine. If the judge cannot reliably tell which is which, the machine passes. To pass, a machine needs natural language processing, knowledge representation, automated reasoning and machine learning.

Narrow, general and super AI

  • Narrow (weak) AI does one task well: face unlock, spam filtering, chess, translation. All AI in real use today is narrow, including large language models, even though they cover many tasks.
  • Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) is a hypothetical system that could learn and perform any intellectual task a human can.
  • Superintelligence is a hypothetical system that is far more capable than humans in almost every area.

A short history of AI

  • 1943 McCulloch & Pitts describe the first mathematical model of a neuron.
  • 1950 Turing proposes the imitation game.
  • 1956 Dartmouth workshop: “artificial intelligence” becomes a field.
  • 1958 Rosenblatt builds the perceptron, a learning neuron.
  • 1966 ELIZA, an early chatbot by Joseph Weizenbaum, imitates a therapist with simple patterns.
  • 1969 Minsky & Papert show a single perceptron cannot learn XOR. Funding cools.
  • 1970s–80s Expert systems (e.g. MYCIN for diagnosing infections) encode human rules. Two “AI winters” follow when promises are not met (around 1974–80 and 1987–93).
  • 1986 Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams popularise backpropagation for training multi-layer networks.
  • 1997 IBM Deep Blue beats world chess champion Garry Kasparov.
  • 2012 AlexNet wins the ImageNet competition by a large margin and starts the deep-learning boom.
  • 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol at Go, 4–1.
  • 2017 “Attention Is All You Need” introduces the Transformer.
  • 2020 AlphaFold 2 predicts protein structures with near-experimental accuracy.
  • 2022 ChatGPT brings large language models to the public.
  • 2024 Nobel Prizes: Physics to Hopfield & Hinton for neural networks; Chemistry partly to Hassabis & Jumper for AlphaFold.

Intelligent Agents

You will learn: what an agent is, how to describe a task with PEAS, the properties of environments, and the five classic types of agents.

The bazaar is loud. A merchant watches customers, checks prices and decides how much to charge.

Tulpar: “Look, Aru, the merchant is an agent. Eyes and ears are his sensors, his hands and voice are his actuators, and profit is his performance measure. I am an agent too, only made of code.”

An agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators. What the agent has perceived so far is its percept sequence. The agent function maps percept sequences to actions; the agent program is the code that implements it.

A rational agent chooses, for every percept sequence, the action that is expected to maximise its performance measure, given what it has perceived and what it knows. Rational does not mean all-knowing. A rational agent can still be unlucky.

Five types of agent programs

  1. Simple reflex agent: acts only on the current percept with condition–action rules. “If the sheep is near the cliff, whistle.”
  2. Model-based reflex agent: keeps an internal state (a model of the world) to handle what it cannot see right now.
  3. Goal-based agent: considers future consequences and picks actions that reach a goal. This needs search and planning.
  4. Utility-based agent: compares how good different outcomes are with a utility function, e.g. fast and safe.
  5. Learning agent: has a learning element that improves the other parts from experience, using feedback from a critic.

Real-world case

A robot vacuum is a model-based agent. Bump and cliff sensors are its percepts; it keeps a map of rooms already cleaned (its internal state) and uses that map to choose where to go next.

Solving Problems by Searching

You will learn: how to formulate a search problem, search trees, and the uninformed strategies BFS, DFS, uniform-cost search and iterative deepening.

Charyn Canyon is a maze of red rock. Somewhere inside is a spring of fresh water.

Aru: “We have no map. How do we find the spring?”

Tulpar: “We search. I don't know which way is closer, so I'll explore systematically. That is called uninformed or blind search.”

Formulating a problem

A search problem has six parts:

  • States: all situations the world can be in (every cell of the canyon).
  • Initial state: where we start (the canyon entrance).
  • Actions: what can be done in a state (move N, S, E, W).
  • Transition model: the state that results from an action.
  • Goal test: is this state a goal? (Is this the spring?)
  • Path cost: the cost of a sequence of actions (number of steps, km, time).

Searching builds a search tree. The root is the initial state; expanding a node generates its children. The set of nodes waiting to be expanded is the frontier. The only difference between strategies is which frontier node is expanded next.

The four uninformed strategies

We judge strategies by four criteria: completeness (always finds a solution if one exists), optimality (finds the cheapest solution), time and space complexity. Here b is the branching factor, d the depth of the shallowest goal and m the maximum depth.

StrategyFrontierComplete?Optimal?TimeSpace
Breadth-first (BFS)FIFO queueYes (finite b)Yes, if all step costs are equalO(bd)O(bd)
Uniform-cost (UCS)Priority queue by path cost g(n)Yes (costs ≥ ε > 0)YesO(b1+⌊C*/ε⌋)
Depth-first (DFS)LIFO stackNo (can loop or go down an infinite path)NoO(bm)O(bm)
Iterative deepening (IDS)DFS with depth limit 0, 1, 2…YesYes, if step costs are equalO(bd)O(bd)

IDS combines the low memory of DFS with the completeness of BFS. Repeating the top levels looks wasteful, but most nodes are on the bottom level, so the extra work is small.

Did you know?

With b = 10 and d = 12, BFS would have to store about 1012 nodes. At 1 KB per node that is about 1 petabyte of memory. Memory, not time, is usually the first wall that BFS hits.

Informed Search, A* and Local Search

You will learn: heuristics, greedy best-first search, A* and when it is optimal, then hill climbing, simulated annealing and genetic algorithms.

From the canyon rim Aru can see the shining spring far away.

Aru: “Now we know roughly where it is. Can't we use that?”

Tulpar: “Yes! A guess about the remaining distance is called a heuristic. With it I can search much smarter.”

A heuristic function h(n) estimates the cost of the cheapest path from node n to the goal. On a grid where we move up/down/left/right, a classic heuristic is the Manhattan distance: |x₁ − x₂| + |y₁ − y₂|.

  • Greedy best-first search expands the node with the smallest h(n). It is often fast but not optimal: it rushes towards the goal and can be fooled by walls.
  • A* search expands the node with the smallest f(n) = g(n) + h(n), where g(n) is the cost already paid to reach n. It balances “how far I've come” with “how far I think is left”.
f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

A heuristic is admissible if it never overestimates the true cost. It is consistent if h(n) ≤ cost(n, n′) + h(n′) for every step. With an admissible heuristic, A* tree search is optimal; with a consistent heuristic, A* graph search is optimal. Manhattan distance is both on our grid, because you can never reach the spring in fewer steps than that.

Local search: climbing the dune

Sometimes the path does not matter, only the final state does: the best timetable, the best layout of a factory, the best weights of a model. Local search keeps just one (or a few) current states and moves to neighbours. It uses very little memory and works on huge spaces.

  • Hill climbing: always move to the best neighbour; stop when no neighbour is better. Fast, but it gets stuck on local maxima, ridges and plateaus. Random-restart hill climbing simply tries again from random starts.
  • Simulated annealing: sometimes accept a worse move, with probability eΔE/T. The “temperature” T starts high (lots of exploring) and slowly cools (more careful), like cooling metal in a forge.
  • Genetic algorithms: keep a population of candidate solutions. Repeatedly select the fittest, cross over pairs to make children and mutate them a little. Inspired by natural evolution.

Real-world case

Navigation apps find routes on road graphs with many millions of nodes. They use A*-style search with good heuristics plus heavy pre-computation (such as contraction hierarchies) so that a route across a country is found in milliseconds.

Adversarial Search and Game Trees

You will learn: types of games, game trees, minimax, alpha-beta pruning, evaluation functions and the horizon effect, expectimax for chance, and (as an extra) constraint satisfaction problems.

In a felt yurt, Ata is playing togyz kumalak, the Kazakh game of nine pits and 162 stones.

Ata: “In the canyon, the rocks did not fight back. Here, every move I make, my opponent answers with the move that hurts me most.”

Tulpar: “Then I must think about his best reply, and my reply to that, and so on. That is minimax.”

Games like chess, Go, togyz kumalak and tic-tac-toe are two-player, zero-sum, deterministic, fully observable games: what one player wins, the other loses. We model them with a game tree: nodes are positions, edges are moves, and leaves have a utility (+1 win, 0 draw, −1 loss).

Minimax

Player MAX wants the highest value, player MIN the lowest. Minimax computes the value of each node bottom-up:

minimax(s) =
  utility(s)                         if s is terminal
  max over actions a of minimax(result(s,a))   if MAX to move
  min over actions a of minimax(result(s,a))   if MIN to move

Alpha-beta pruning

Minimax explores the whole tree, which grows as O(bm). Alpha-beta pruning keeps two numbers: α, the best value MAX can already guarantee, and β, the best value MIN can guarantee. When a branch cannot change the decision (α ≥ β), it is cut off. The result is exactly the same as minimax. With perfect move ordering the cost drops to about O(bm/2), so the program can look about twice as deep in the same time.

Real games are too big to search to the end: chess has around 10120 possible games (Shannon's estimate). Programs stop at a depth limit and use an evaluation function, a heuristic score of a position. Usually it is a weighted sum of features:

Eval(s) = w₁·f₁(s) + w₂·f₂(s) + … + wₙ·fₙ(s)

In chess, f₁ might be the material difference and f₂ the number of legal moves. Unlike an A* heuristic, which estimates a cost to the goal, an evaluation function estimates who is winning. A fixed depth limit causes the horizon effect: a disaster that lies just one move beyond the limit is invisible, so the program may make a move that only delays the loss.

Expectimax: when dice play too

In backgammon or in a dice-based game, the next state depends on chance, not only on an opponent. Expectimax replaces MIN nodes with chance nodes whose value is the expected value (probability-weighted average) of their children:

V(chance node) = Σ P(outcome) · V(outcome)

Example: a move leads to a coin flip with outcomes worth 10 and 0 (each 50%), so its value is 5. Another move gives a sure 4. Expectimax picks the coin flip (5 > 4), while minimax, which assumes the worst outcome, would pick the sure 4. Use minimax for a smart adversary and expectimax for randomness or an opponent who plays carelessly. Because chance nodes need all children, alpha-beta pruning does not apply directly.

Constraint satisfaction problems (CSP)

Ata: “After the tournament there is a toi, a feast. Guests who argue must not sit at the same table. Can your machine plan the seating?”

A CSP has variables X₁…Xₙ, a domain of possible values for each, and constraints that say which combinations are allowed. A solution assigns every variable a value with no constraint violated. Examples: map colouring, Sudoku, exam timetables, seating plans.

CSPs are solved with backtracking search: assign one variable at a time and undo (backtrack) as soon as a constraint is broken. Smart tricks make it much faster:

  • MRV (minimum remaining values): choose the variable with the fewest legal values left (“fail first”).
  • Degree heuristic: break ties by choosing the variable involved in the most constraints.
  • Least constraining value: try the value that rules out the fewest options for neighbours.
  • Forward checking and arc consistency (AC-3): after each assignment, remove values from neighbours' domains that can no longer work, and detect dead ends early.

Markov Decision Processes and Reinforcement Learning

You will learn: MDPs, return and discounting, V and Q, the Bellman equation, value and policy iteration, Monte Carlo and TD learning, Q-learning, SARSA, exploration, and deep RL.

A berkutchi, an eagle hunter, trains a golden eagle. He never explains the rules. When the eagle does well, it gets meat; when it fails, it gets nothing.

Tulpar: “That is how I can learn to act too: by trial, error and reward.”

In reinforcement learning (RL) an agent interacts with an environment. At each step it observes a state s, takes an action a, receives a reward r and moves to a new state s′. Its goal is to learn a policy π(s) that maximises the total discounted reward r₀ + γr₁ + γ²r₂ + …, where the discount factor 0 ≤ γ < 1 makes near rewards worth more than far ones.

Search (Chapters 3–4) assumed every action has a certain result. In the steppe, a gust of wind can push the eagle off course. For sequential decisions under uncertainty we use a Markov decision process (MDP):

  • S: set of states; A: set of actions
  • P(s′ | s, a): transition probabilities (actions can have random results)
  • R(s, a, s′): reward for each transition
  • γ: discount factor. With γ = 0.9, a reward of 10 that arrives in 3 steps is worth 10 · 0.9³ ≈ 7.3 today.

“Markov” means the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on the full history. Note that reward ≠ value: the reward is the immediate signal, the value is the total discounted return expected from now on.

Value functions and the Bellman equation

  • Vπ(s), the state-value: expected return starting in s and following policy π.
  • Qπ(s, a), the action-value: expected return after doing a in s, then following π. They are linked by V(s) = maxa Q(s, a) for the optimal policy.

The Bellman optimality equation says the value of a state is the best immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you end up:

V*(s) = maxₐ Σₛ′ P(s′|s,a) · [ R(s,a,s′) + γ · V*(s′) ]

Planning when the model is known

  • Value iteration: start with V = 0 everywhere and apply the Bellman equation to every state again and again until the values stop changing. Then take the best action in each state.
  • Policy iteration: alternate policy evaluation (compute Vπ for the current policy) and policy improvement (make the policy greedy with respect to Vπ) until the policy stops changing. It often needs fewer iterations.

If P and R are known, this is planning (model-based). If they are unknown, the agent must act, observe and learn: that is reinforcement learning (RL). Unlike supervised learning, nobody gives the correct action; the agent gets only a reward, often delayed.

Learning from experience: Monte Carlo and TD

  • Monte Carlo: play a whole episode, then average the actual returns seen from each state.
  • Temporal-Difference (TD) learning: update after every step using the current estimate of the next state: V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s′) − V(s)]. TD learns faster and does not need the episode to end.

Q-learning

Q-learning (Watkins, 1989) learns Q(s, a): the expected future reward of doing action a in state s and acting well afterwards. After every step it nudges the estimate:

Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α [ r + γ · maxₐ′ Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ]

α is the learning rate. The part in brackets is the “surprise”: the difference between what happened and what the agent expected. Q-learning does not need a model of the environment (it is model-free).

A complete numerical update: Q(s,a) = 2, reward r = −1, the best Q in the next state is 6, α = 0.5, γ = 0.9. Target = −1 + 0.9·6 = 4.4. New Q = 2 + 0.5·(4.4 − 2) = 3.2.

Three numbers are easy to mix up: α (learning rate: how far to move towards the new estimate), γ (discount: how much the future matters) and ε (exploration rate).

Exploration vs exploitation: should the eagle use the best route it knows (exploit) or try something new that might be better (explore)? A common answer is ε-greedy: with probability ε pick a random action, otherwise the best known one.

SARSA is a close relative. It updates with the action the agent actually takes next, Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α[r + γQ(s′,a′) − Q(s,a)], so it is on-policy, while Q-learning, which uses the max, is off-policy. Near a cliff, SARSA learns a safer path because it accounts for its own random exploring moves.

Beyond tables

A Q-table needs one row per state, which is impossible for images or large worlds. Deep Q-Networks (DQN) replace the table with a neural network that estimates Q(s, a). Policy-gradient methods learn the policy directly, and actor–critic methods combine both: the actor chooses actions, the critic estimates their value. In real deployments (battery energy management, predictive maintenance of machines) RL also needs safety limits, careful state design and testing in simulation first.

Reasoning under Uncertainty

You will learn: probability basics, conditional probability, Bayes' rule, the base-rate trap, naive Bayes, Bayesian networks, chains, forks and colliders, exact inference and sampling.

A vet's quick test says one of Ata's lambs has a rare illness.

Aru: “The test is 90% accurate, so the lamb is 90% likely to be sick, right?”

Tulpar: “Not at all! Logic said true or false. The real world says probably. Let me reason with probabilities.”

Real agents rarely know everything: sensors are noisy and the world is random. Probability gives a degree of belief between 0 and 1.

  • Prior P(A): belief before seeing evidence.
  • Conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A ∧ B) / P(B): belief in A once we know B.
  • Independence: A and B are independent if P(A | B) = P(A).

Bayes' rule

P(Sick | +) = P(+ | Sick) · P(Sick) / P(+)

where P(+) = P(+ | Sick)·P(Sick) + P(+ | Healthy)·P(Healthy). Bayes' rule turns “how likely is the evidence if the cause is true” into “how likely is the cause given the evidence”. It is the main tool for updating beliefs.

Experiment 4 · 1,000 lambs and one test

Each dot is one lamb. Red = sick and tests positive, gold = healthy but tests positive (false alarm), faded = tests negative. Move the sliders.

With the default numbers, only about 9% of positive lambs are really sick, because healthy lambs are so much more common that their false alarms outnumber the true cases. Ignoring the prior like this is called the base-rate fallacy, and doctors, judges and AI engineers all have to watch out for it.

Naive Bayes and Bayesian networks

A naive Bayes classifier assumes every feature is independent given the class: P(Class | f₁…fₙ) ∝ P(Class) · ∏ P(fᵢ | Class). The assumption is “naive”, but it works well for text. Early spam filters used it: words like “prize” and “click” raise P(spam).

A Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph where each node is a random variable with a conditional probability table (CPT) given its parents. The full joint distribution factorises into a product of these local tables:

P(X₁, …, Xₙ) = Π P(Xᵢ | Parents(Xᵢ))

This saves a lot of space. A full joint table over 30 yes/no variables needs 2³⁰ − 1 ≈ 1 billion numbers. If each variable has at most 3 parents, the network needs at most 30 · 2³ = 240.

Three basic shapes

ShapeExampleWhat happens
Chain A → B → CStorm → Sheep scattered → Ata worriedA and C are linked, but once B is known, A tells us nothing more about C.
Fork A ← B → CDog barks ← Sheep scattered → Ata worriedA common cause links the two effects; knowing B makes them independent.
Collider A → B ← CStorm → Sheep scattered ← WolfA and C are independent, until B is observed. Then they become linked.

The collider produces explaining away: if the sheep are scattered and we learn there was a storm, our belief that a wolf came goes down, because the storm already explains the evidence.

Inference: exact and approximate

  • Exact inference by enumeration: to get P(Query | evidence), sum the joint probability over all values of the hidden variables, then normalise. It is exact but grows exponentially with the number of hidden variables.
  • Sampling is counting: generate many random “worlds” from the network, top to bottom, following the CPTs.
  • Rejection sampling: throw away samples that disagree with the evidence and count the rest. Simple, but wasteful when the evidence is rare.
  • Likelihood weighting: fix the evidence variables to their observed values and give each sample a weight equal to how likely that evidence was. No samples are wasted.

Real-world case

Predictive maintenance: a network links hidden causes (bearing wear, overheating) to sensor readings (vibration, temperature, alarm). Given today's readings, inference gives the probability of each fault, so engineers can repair a machine before it breaks.

Hidden Markov Models

You will learn: hidden states vs. observations, Markov chains and transition matrices, emission tables, filtering, prediction, smoothing and the Viterbi algorithm.

The camp's water comes from an old pump. Nobody can see inside it, but a small sensor shows its temperature: Normal or High.

Aru: “The sensor says High. So the pump is broken?”

Tulpar: “Not necessarily. Sensors give false alarms, and a fault can exist before any alarm. The pump's true condition is hidden. I have to reason about it over time.”

Many problems have a hidden state that changes over time and produces noisy observations: a machine's health and its sensor readings, the words someone says and the sound we hear, the weather and whether people carry umbrellas.

Markov chains

A Markov chain describes how a state changes from one time step to the next. The Markov assumption says the next state depends only on the current one:

P(Xₜ₊₁ | Xₜ, Xₜ₋₁, …) = P(Xₜ₊₁ | Xₜ)

The changes are stored in a transition matrix T; each row adds up to 1.

Now ↓ / next →HealthyFaulty
Healthy0.900.10
Faulty0.200.80

To predict one step ahead, multiply the belief vector by T: if today P = [0.80, 0.20], then tomorrow P(Healthy) = 0.80·0.90 + 0.20·0.20 = 0.76 and P(Faulty) = 0.80·0.10 + 0.20·0.80 = 0.24.

The three ingredients of an HMM

  1. Initial probabilities π: P(Healthy) = 0.80, P(Faulty) = 0.20 at the start.
  2. Transition probabilities T: the table above.
  3. Emission probabilities O: how likely each reading is in each state.
State ↓ / reading →NormalHigh
Healthy0.800.20
Faulty0.300.70

Careful: P(High | Faulty) = 0.70 is not P(Faulty | High). To reverse it we need Bayes' rule from the previous chapter.

Filtering: what is happening now?

Filtering estimates the current hidden state from all readings so far. Each step has four parts: predict (move the belief forward with T), observe a reading, correct (multiply by the emission likelihood) and normalise (make it sum to 1).

beliefₜ(j) ∝ Oⱼ(eₜ) · Σᵢ beliefₜ₋₁(i) · Tᵢⱼ

Worked example with readings High, High:

  • Time 1 (no transition yet): Healthy 0.80·0.20 = 0.16, Faulty 0.20·0.70 = 0.14. Normalise by 0.30 → [53.3%, 46.7%].
  • Time 2, predict: Healthy 0.533·0.90 + 0.467·0.20 ≈ 0.573; Faulty ≈ 0.427.
  • Time 2, correct: [0.573·0.20, 0.427·0.70] = [0.115, 0.299]. Normalise by 0.413 → [27.7%, 72.3%].

Prediction looks into the future with no new readings: pₜ₊ₖ = pₜ · Tᵏ. One step after [0.277, 0.723] gives Healthy ≈ 39.4%.

Smoothing and Viterbi: what happened before?

Smoothing re-estimates a past state using later readings too, with the forward–backward method: P(Xₜ | e₁…e_T) ∝ forwardₜ × backwardₜ. In our example, after the second High, the estimate that the pump was already Faulty at time 1 rises from 46.7% to 67.7%, because a second High is much more likely if the pump was faulty earlier (0.60 vs. 0.25).

The Viterbi algorithm answers a different question: which complete sequence of hidden states is most likely? It uses dynamic programming: at each step it keeps only the best path into each state. For High, High the four paths score HH = 0.0288, HF = 0.0112, FH = 0.0056, FF = 0.0784, so the most likely story is Faulty, Faulty.

Add sensor readings one by one. For each time step Tulpar shows the filtered belief (bar) and, at the bottom, the Viterbi path: the single most likely sequence of hidden states. The model uses the tables above.

Real-world case

HMMs powered speech recognition for decades (hidden words, observed sounds). They are still used for gene finding in DNA, part-of-speech tagging, and machine-health monitoring.

Part 1 · Rational decisions

Winter is coming. Ata must decide: repair the well pump now, or wait and hope it survives until spring?

Tulpar: “A rational choice is not a guaranteed good outcome. It is the action with the best expected result, given what we know now.”

A rational agent uses the evidence available now and handles uncertainty explicitly. It can still be unlucky. An omniscient agent would know the real outcome in advance, which is impossible. So we judge a decision by its expected outcome, not by what happened afterwards.

Probability alone is not enough: a 90% chance of success says nothing about how bad the other 10% is. A utility gives each outcome a preference score. The expected utility of an action weights each outcome's utility by its probability:

EU(a) = Σᵢ P(oᵢ | a) · U(oᵢ) a* = argmaxₐ EU(a)

This is the principle of Maximum Expected Utility (MEU). Worked example:

OptionSuccessFailureExpected utility
Project A90% × (+100)10% × (−20)0.9·100 + 0.1·(−20) = 88
Project B60% × (+200)40% × (−50)0.6·200 + 0.4·(−50) = 100

Project B is riskier, yet it is the rational choice on this utility scale. People are not always risk neutral: a risk-averse person (concave utility) dislikes big losses and might prefer A, while a risk-seeking person accepts uncertainty for a chance of a big win.

From belief to action: the maintenance threshold

Bayes' rule (Chapter 7) turns an alarm into an updated probability of failure. Utilities then turn that probability into an action. If continuing costs pfailure · Cfailure on average, and maintenance costs Cmaint and prevents the failure:

Maintain if p_failure > C_maint / C_failure

Example from the lecture: failure probability 15%, failure loss $20,000, maintenance $2,000. Expected cost of waiting = 0.15 × 20,000 = $3,000 > $2,000, so repair now.

Move the sliders. Tulpar compares the expected cost of waiting with the cost of repairing and picks the cheaper action.

Part 2 · Why learn from data?

In traditional programming a person writes the rules: IF temperature > 80 THEN alarm. Data + rules → answers. But real data are noisy: a single hand-written threshold cannot separate overlapping “healthy” and “faulty” readings. In machine learning, data + answers → rules: the algorithm learns a model ŷ = fθ(x), where x are the input features and θ the learned parameters.

The ML workflow: collect labelled examples → split into training and test data → fit the parameters → evaluate on unseen data → decide and act.

Tom Mitchell's classic definition: a program learns from experience E with respect to task T and performance measure P if its performance at T, measured by P, improves with E.

TypeDataGoalExample
SupervisedInputs with correct labelsLearn a mapping input → labelPhoto → “fat-tailed” or “merino”; house features → price
UnsupervisedInputs only, no labelsFind structure: groups, patternsGroup customers by behaviour
ReinforcementRewards from actingLearn a policy that maximises rewardTraining an eagle or a game bot (Chapter 6)

The same machine data can support all three: supervised (with failure labels, predict “will this machine fail?”), unsupervised (without labels, discover groups of normal and unusual behaviour) and reinforcement (learn when to schedule maintenance from rewards).

Supervised learning has two main tasks: regression predicts a continuous number (energy demand, price, lifetime; measured with MAE, RMSE, R²) and classification predicts a class or a probability (failure, fraud, spam; measured with accuracy, precision, recall, F1).

Linear regression and gradient descent

Linear regression fits a line ŷ = w·x + b. We measure error with the mean squared error and minimise it with gradient descent: repeatedly take a small step (the learning rate η) against the gradient, the direction in which error grows fastest.

MSE = (1/n) Σ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² w ← w − η · ∂MSE/∂w

Logistic regression: from score to probability

Despite its name, logistic regression is a classifier. It computes a linear score z = wᵀx + b and passes it through the sigmoid function, which turns any number into a probability between 0 and 1:

σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)) P(y = 1 | x) = σ(wᵀx + b)
z−2−1012
σ(z)11.9%26.9%50%73.1%88.1%

We predict “failure” if P(failure | x) ≥ τ. The default threshold τ = 0.5 is not always best: when a missed failure is very expensive, the rational threshold is lower (see the maintenance rule above).

Decision trees

A decision tree asks questions about features until it reaches a leaf with an answer. To choose the best question, ID3/C4.5 use entropy and information gain; CART uses Gini impurity.

H(S) = − Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ Gain(S, A) = H(S) − Σ (|Sᵥ|/|S|) · H(Sᵥ)

Example: a flock of 8 sheep with 4 of each breed has H = 1 bit (maximum uncertainty). If the question “Has a fat tail?” splits it into two pure groups, both children have H = 0, so the gain is 1 bit, the best possible question.

The lecture's machine example works the same way: Temperature > 70 °C? If no → Normal. If yes → Vibration > 3? If no → Normal, if yes → Failure. Trees are interpretable, model non-linear relationships and need little preprocessing, but deep trees overfit and small changes in data can change the whole tree.

A random forest fixes much of this: (1) bootstrap: train each tree on a random sample of rows; (2) random features: each split considers only a random subset of features; (3) train many diverse trees; (4) aggregate: vote (classification) or average (regression). The forest usually generalises better than one tree, but it is harder to explain.

k-nearest neighbours (k-NN)

To classify a new sheep, find the k most similar sheep in the training data (by a distance such as Euclidean) and take a majority vote. There is no training phase, but prediction can be slow on large data. Small k follows noise; large k smooths too much.

k-means clustering (unsupervised)

  1. Choose k and place k centroids randomly.
  2. Assign: each point joins its nearest centroid.
  3. Update: move each centroid to the mean of its points.
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 until nothing changes.

k-means minimises the within-cluster sum of squared distances (the SSE shown in the experiment):

J = Σₖ Σ_(x ∈ Cₖ) ‖x − μₖ‖²

Other unsupervised tasks are dimensionality reduction and anomaly detection.

Overfitting, data splits and evaluation

A model that memorises training data, noise included, overfits: it scores well on training data and badly on new data. A model too simple to capture the pattern underfits. This is the bias–variance trade-off. To detect overfitting we split data into a training set (to learn), a validation set (to tune settings, or use k-fold cross-validation) and a test set (touched only once, at the end). Regularisation, more data and simpler models all reduce overfitting.

Predicted: sickPredicted: healthy
Actually sickTrue positive (TP) = 40False negative (FN) = 10
Actually healthyFalse positive (FP) = 20True negative (TN) = 930
  • Accuracy = (TP + TN) / all = 970 / 1000 = 97%
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 40 / 60 ≈ 67% (of lambs flagged sick, how many really are)
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 40 / 50 = 80% (of sick lambs, how many we caught)
  • F1 = 2 · P · R / (P + R) ≈ 73%

Myth vs fact

Myth: 97% accuracy means the model is great.
Fact: If only 5% of lambs are sick, a model that always says “healthy” already gets 95% accuracy while catching zero sick lambs. On unbalanced data, look at precision and recall.

From prediction to rational action

The two halves of this chapter fit together: the model predicts P(failure | sensors), a cost model says what each outcome costs, and the agent chooses the action with the lowest expected cost:

a* = argminₐ Σₒ P(o | x, a) · C(o, a)

This is why high accuracy ≠ good decisions. Model A: 95% accuracy but 60% failure recall. Model B: 92% accuracy but 90% recall. If missed failures are expensive, Model B is the better choice. Other risks: data leakage (training on information not available at prediction time), distribution shift (new conditions differ from training data) and poor calibration (predicted probabilities that do not match real frequencies).

Perceptrons and Neural Networks

You will learn: the artificial neuron and perceptron, how a perceptron learns from an error, linear separability, hidden layers and activation functions, gradient descent and backpropagation, train/validation/test, overfitting and generalisation, CNNs and modern deep learning.

At Baikonur, where Yuri Gagarin's rocket launched in 1961, engineers are testing a camera that spots damaged rocket tiles.

Tulpar: “Nobody can write rules for every crack. So the engineers built a network of simple units, a bit like neurons in a brain, and let it learn from photos.”

The artificial neuron

An artificial neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds a bias, and passes the sum through an activation function:

output = f( w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + … + wₙxₙ + b )

Common activations: step (perceptron), sigmoid 1/(1 + e−z) (squashes to 0…1), tanh, and ReLU max(0, z), the default in modern deep networks. Without a non-linear activation, stacking layers would still only give a linear function.

Worked example: is the ground safe for the robot?

A mobile robot must classify a surface as STABLE (1) or UNSTABLE (0), a classification task, from two sensors: Grip (how well the wheels grip) and Slip (how much they slide). The perceptron uses a step activation: output 1 if z ≥ 0, otherwise 0.

z = w₁·Grip + w₂·Slip + b, with w₁ = +2, w₂ = −3, b = −0.5
  • Grip = 0.8, Slip = 0.2: z = 2(0.8) − 3(0.2) − 0.5 = 1.6 − 0.6 − 0.5 = +0.5 → STABLE
  • Grip = 0.8, Slip = 0.8: z = 1.6 − 2.4 − 0.5 = −1.3 → UNSTABLE

Learning from an error. Suppose that second surface was actually STABLE (y = 1). The error is y − ŷ = 1 − 0 = +1. With learning rate η = 0.1 the rule wnew = wold + η·(y − ŷ)·x gives:

  • Grip weight: 2 + 0.1·1·0.8 = 2.08
  • Slip weight: −3 + 0.1·1·0.8 = −2.92
  • Bias: −0.5 + 0.1·1 = −0.4

Weights decide how much each input matters; the bias shifts the threshold. Each correction nudges the boundary towards the right answer.

The perceptron and the XOR problem

Rosenblatt's perceptron learns with a simple rule: after each example, w ← w + η (y − ŷ) x. It is guaranteed to converge if the classes can be separated by a straight line. AND and OR can be; XOR cannot. Try it:

Experiment 8 · Train a single neuron

Four points: filled = output 1, hollow = output 0. Pick a logic gate and train. The line is the neuron's decision boundary.

Multi-layer networks and backpropagation

Adding a hidden layer solves XOR: the hidden neurons draw two lines, and the output neuron combines them. A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) has an input layer, one or more hidden layers and an output layer. In theory, one hidden layer with enough neurons can approximate any continuous function (the universal approximation theorem).

Activation functions add non-linearity: without them, a deep network would still draw only a straight boundary. Training uses gradient descent on a loss L, where the learning rate η controls the step size:

w_new = w_old − η · ∂L/∂w

Backpropagation trains the network. (1) Forward pass: compute the output. (2) Compute the loss, e.g. cross-entropy for classification. (3) Backward pass: use the chain rule of calculus to find how much each weight contributed to the error. (4) Update every weight with gradient descent. One pass over the whole training set is an epoch; in practice we update on small mini-batches (stochastic gradient descent).

Does it generalise, or just memorise?

Use three separate sets: train (learn the weights), validate (choose settings and compare models) and test (final check on unseen data). The same example must never be in both training and test sets.

Training accuracyTest accuracyVerdict
Model A98%65%Overfitting: memorised the training data
Model B91%89%Generalises: trust this one

It is like a student who memorises 18 answers and scores 18/18 in practice, then gets only 2/6 on new questions. Watch the loss curves: if training loss keeps falling while validation loss starts rising, the network is starting to overfit.

Not all mistakes cost the same. For the robot, predicting STABLE when the ground is actually UNSTABLE is dangerous; the opposite is only a false alarm. A safety-critical system should be tuned for high recall on the dangerous class. Always compare with a simple baseline (in the lecture's illustration, a decision tree reached 82% accuracy and F1 0.79 in 1 second; the neural network 88% and 0.86 in 12 seconds), and inspect individual errors instead of only counting them.

from sklearn.neural_network import MLPClassifier
model = MLPClassifier(hidden_layer_sizes=(3,), random_state=42, max_iter=1000)
model.fit(X_train, y_train)
predictions = model.predict(X_test)
accuracy = model.score(X_test, y_test)

Memorising, learning, understanding

A network that recognises 100 cat photos it has seen has memorised. One that recognises a new cat in a different pose or light has learned patterns: it generalises. Whether it understands what a cat is remains an open question. High accuracy alone does not prove understanding.

Deep architectures

  • Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) go from pixels → edges and textures → shapes and patterns → a class such as “cat”. They slide small filters over an image to detect edges, then shapes, then objects. Weight sharing and pooling make them efficient for vision. LeNet (1998) read handwritten digits; AlexNet (2012) started the deep-learning boom.
  • Recurrent neural networks (RNNs, LSTMs) process sequences step by step with a memory of what came before. They were used for speech and translation, but struggle with very long sequences.
  • Transformers (2017) use self-attention: every word looks at every other word and decides which ones matter. They train in parallel and scale well.
  • Large language models (LLMs) such as GPT, Claude and Gemini are huge Transformers trained to predict the next token on large amounts of text, then fine-tuned (e.g. with human feedback) to be helpful assistants.
  • Generative AI also includes diffusion models, which create images by learning to remove noise step by step.

Did you know?

A “parameter” is one weight or bias. LeNet-5 had about 60,000 parameters; AlexNet about 60 million; today's largest language models have hundreds of billions.

Myth vs fact

Myth: Neural networks work just like the human brain.
Fact: They were inspired by neurons, but real neurons are far more complex, the brain does not learn with backpropagation as far as we know, and a human brain uses about 20 watts.

Logic and Knowledge Representation

You will learn: knowledge-based agents, propositional logic, truth tables, entailment, inference rules, forward and backward chaining, first-order logic and expert systems.

Among the ruins of Otrar, the city of al-Farabi, Aru finds old pages about reasoning.

Tulpar: “Search tells me how to reach a goal. Logic lets me know things and prove new things from what I know.”

A knowledge-based agent has a knowledge base (KB): a set of sentences in a formal language. It can TELL the KB new facts and ASK it questions. An inference engine derives new sentences from old ones.

Propositional logic

Sentences are built from propositions (statements that are true or false, like Rain) and connectives:

PQ¬P (not)P ∧ Q (and)P ∨ Q (or)P ⇒ Q (implies)P ⇔ Q (iff)
FFTFFTT
FTTFTTF
TFFFTFF
TTFTTTT

Note the surprise in the implication column: “P ⇒ Q” is only false when P is true and Q is false. “If it rains, the steppe is wet” is not broken on a dry, sunny day.

A model is one assignment of true/false to every proposition. KB entails α (written KB ⊨ α) if α is true in every model where KB is true. An inference procedure is sound if it only derives entailed sentences, and complete if it can derive every entailed sentence.

Inference rules

  • Modus ponens: from P ⇒ Q and P, conclude Q.
  • And-elimination: from P ∧ Q, conclude P.
  • Resolution: from (P ∨ Q) and (¬Q ∨ R), conclude (P ∨ R). Resolution alone is complete for propositional logic when sentences are in conjunctive normal form (CNF).
KB:  Clouds ∧ Wind ⇒ Storm
     Storm ⇒ BringSheepHome
     Clouds
     Wind
Forward chaining:
  Clouds, Wind        ⇒ Storm            (modus ponens)
  Storm               ⇒ BringSheepHome   (modus ponens)
Answer: bring the sheep home.

Forward chaining starts from known facts and fires rules until nothing new appears (data-driven). Backward chaining starts from the question and works back to find facts that support it (goal-driven, used in Prolog).

First-order logic (FOL)

Propositional logic cannot say “all sheep eat grass” without one proposition per sheep. First-order logic adds objects, predicates, functions, variables and quantifiers:

∀x  Sheep(x) ⇒ EatsGrass(x)        "every sheep eats grass"
∃x  Eagle(x) ∧ Hunts(x, Fox)       "some eagle hunts a fox"
Sheep(Akbas)                       "Akbas is a sheep"
⊢   EatsGrass(Akbas)               (universal instantiation + modus ponens)

Real-world case

Expert systems store expert knowledge as IF–THEN rules plus an inference engine. MYCIN (Stanford, 1970s) used about 600 rules to suggest antibiotics for blood infections and performed about as well as specialists in tests. Expert systems are easy to explain but hard to maintain, because every rule must be written by hand.

Myth vs fact

Myth: Modern AI no longer uses logic.
Fact: Logic lives on in database query engines, chip and software verification, planning systems, knowledge graphs, and rule engines for law and tax. Research on “neuro-symbolic” AI combines logic with neural networks.

Ethics, Safety and the Future of AI

You will learn: the main ethical risks of AI, principles for responsible AI, how regulation works, and how AI compares with human intelligence.

At the end of the journey, Ata looks at Tulpar for a long time.

Ata: “A strong horse without a good rider is dangerous. You are clever now, Tulpar. But are you fair? Are you honest? Who answers for your mistakes?”

Aru: “Those questions are our job, Ata. Not only the machine's.”

Key risks

  • Bias and fairness. Models learn from historical data and can copy its unfairness. The Gender Shades study (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018) found commercial face-analysis systems had error rates up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women but under 1% for lighter-skinned men.
  • Privacy. Training data and face recognition can expose personal information.
  • Transparency and explainability. Deep networks are “black boxes”. Explainable AI (XAI) methods such as feature-importance maps try to show why a model decided something.
  • Accountability. If a self-driving car crashes, who is responsible: the maker, the programmer or the owner?
  • Misinformation. Deepfakes and generated text make false content cheap. LLMs can also “hallucinate”: state false things confidently.
  • Jobs. AI automates some tasks and creates new ones. Workers need re-skilling.
  • Safety and alignment. Making sure powerful systems actually pursue the goals people intend, and stay under meaningful human control.

Principles and regulation

The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021), adopted by all 193 member states, calls for human rights, fairness, transparency, human oversight and sustainability. The EU AI Act (2024) is the first broad AI law. It sorts systems by risk: unacceptable (banned, e.g. social scoring), high (strict rules, e.g. hiring, medical devices), limited (transparency duties, e.g. chatbots must say they are AI) and minimal (e.g. spam filters, no extra rules).
